Adaptation of Satellite Land-Cover Classification to Indian Geospatial
Conditions}}

\title{Edge-Native Active Learning for Category-Sensitive Domain Shift in Geospatial Classification}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Saim Jawed}\\
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Department of Computer Science}}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Current approaches to geospatial land-cover classification rely on massive vision transformers (ViTs) and Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA) pipelines executed in centralized cloud environments. However, these models suffer from catastrophic degradation when applied to out-of-distribution geographies, a phenomenon known as Category-Sensitive Domain Shift. For example, models trained on European datasets (EuroSAT) frequently misclassify highly polluted Indian water bodies as dense forests. In this paper, we propose GeoScan, an edge-native Active Learning framework designed to resolve category-sensitive domain shift via human-in-the-loop (HITL) fine-tuning. Unlike Source-Free UDA or parameter-heavy ViT architectures, GeoScan deploys an int8-quantized EfficientNetV2 model directly to the client browser via WebAssembly, utilizing Shannon Entropy to flag uncertain predictions in real-time. By dynamically routing high-entropy patches to an interactive coordinate-mapping UI, we allow domain experts to correct localized biases. Our empirical results demonstrate that this targeted active learning methodology rapidly corrects domain-specific catastrophic failures (improving F1 scores from 0.08 to 0.77 on water bodies) while drastically reducing the computational and financial overhead required by traditional cloud-based UDA pipelines.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Domain Shift, Active Learning, Edge AI, EfficientNetV2, Geospatial Analysis, WebAssembly
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Satellite imagery provides a wealth of information for urban planning, environmental monitoring, and disaster response. Deep learning, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers (ViTs), has achieved state-of-the-art results on benchmark land-cover datasets such as EuroSAT. However, deploying these models in real-world, global contexts exposes a critical vulnerability: Category-Sensitive Domain Shift. 

Formally, domain shift occurs when the source distribution $P^s(\mathbf{x}, y)$ differs significantly from the target distribution $P^t(\mathbf{x}, y)$. In geospatial analysis, this shift is heavily localized and category-dependent. For instance, a model trained to recognize the pristine blue lakes of Europe will catastrophically fail when confronted with the algae-rich, turbid rivers of the Indian subcontinent, frequently misclassifying them as vegetation.

Current methodologies attempt to solve this via Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA) or by utilizing massive foundational models (e.g., ViTs). While effective in controlled benchmarks, these approaches require substantial centralized compute, creating a financial bottleneck for continuous, global-scale geospatial inference. 

We argue that the solution to localized domain shift is not larger models, but rather targeted, computationally cheap Active Learning at the edge. We introduce GeoScan, a system that shifts the computational burden from the cloud to the client via WebAssembly (Wasm) and ONNX Runtime. By evaluating the Shannon Entropy of the softmax distribution of an int8-quantized EfficientNetV2 model, GeoScan actively flags out-of-distribution patches for human correction.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Vision Transformers and Geospatial Benchmarks}
Recent advancements have heavily favored Vision Transformers for geospatial tasks. The TSP RIG framework demonstrates state-of-the-art performance utilizing ViTs trained on the EuroSAT benchmark. However, the parameter density of ViTs (often exceeding 80M parameters) restricts their deployment to high-end cloud GPUs. Our work directly contrasts this by utilizing an edge-native, quantized EfficientNetV2 (5.9M parameters), sacrificing a marginal percentage of theoretical benchmark accuracy for the ability to run directly in a client's browser, enabling real-time edge inference.

\subsection{Domain Adaptation in Remote Sensing}
To combat distribution shifts, Fang et al. proposed Category-Sensitive Domain Adaptation (CsDA), while Mohammadi et al. introduced Source-Free Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (SFUDA). Both methodologies rely on complex adversarial networks or pseudo-labeling mechanisms that require continuous cloud computation to align feature spaces. While theoretically robust, these methods are financially prohibitive for global-scale scanning. GeoScan circumvents the computational overhead of UDA by relying on targeted Active Learning, correcting the feature space explicitly through minimal, high-value human interventions rather than expensive algorithmic alignment.

\subsection{Active Learning and Human-in-the-Loop}
The theoretical foundation of using human annotations to resolve model uncertainty was explored by Persello (2013) and recently surveyed by Lyu (2025). GeoScan modernizes this paradigm by embedding the uncertainty heuristic (Shannon Entropy) directly into the client-side architecture via WebAssembly, eliminating the latency of server-side inference and allowing domain experts to interactively correct localized domain shifts in real-time.

\subsection{How a Session Works}

An expert drops in a GPS coordinate. The quadtree instantly maps it to a
$\sim$640 m $\times$ 640 m leaf cell, expands that to an $8 \times 4$ grid,
and hits the GEE serverless function. GEE hands back a $1{,}792 \times 896$
JPEG. The browser takes over, running a sliding window to pull 55
overlapping $224 \times 224$ patches. EfficientNetV2 evaluates each one,
we accumulate the softmax probabilities, and a smooth heatmap appears on
the screen. Click a patch, and you see its entropy. If the AI blew it,
the expert corrects the label and fires it off to Firestore. Not a
single pixel gets saved to a database.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{The Virtual Quadtree}

We start with a massive bounding box covering the entire Indian
subcontinent:

\begin{verbatim}
ROOT: {minLat: 0.0, maxLat: 48.0, minLon: 60.0, maxLon: 108.0}
\end{verbatim}

Cut it in half along both axes. Do that 13 times. You get:

\begin{verbatim}
Leaf width  = 48 / 2^13 = 0.00586 deg longitude  ~ 591 m at 25 deg N
Leaf height = 48 / 2^13 = 0.00586 deg latitude   ~ 652 m
\end{verbatim}

This perfectly aligns with EuroSAT's native $64 \times 64$ pixel scale
($640\,\text{m} \times 640\,\text{m}$ at 10 m/px). The whole tree spans
67,108,864 cells. We can resolve any coordinate to its leaf with 13
simple binary checks.

\subsection{Pulling Sentinel-2 Imagery}

\begin{equation}
R_z = \frac{C \cdot \cos(\phi)}{2^{z+8}}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $C$ is the equatorial circumference and $\phi$ is the latitude. We extract imagery at a fixed depth $z=13$, ensuring a standardized patch size of $64 \times 64$ pixels representing approximately $10 \text{m}/\text{pixel}$. Patches are extracted via the Google Earth Engine API using a bounding box $\mathcal{B} = \{lat_{min}, lon_{min}, lat_{max}, lon_{max}\}$.


We stick to visible RGB (B4, B3, B2) to match the EuroSAT training data.
The gamma bump of 1.4 just fixes the dark, low-reflectance look you
usually get from atmospherically corrected surface data over vegetation.
The image comes back sized at $1{,}792 \times 896$ pixels so it tiles
cleanly into the 224-pixel CNN inputs.

\subsection{Setting Up the Baseline}

We trained EfficientNetV2-Small on EuroSAT RGB, squishing the original
10 classes down to 6:

\toprule\noalign{}

\midrule\noalign{}

AnnualCrop, PermanentCrop & Crop & 0 \\
Forest & Forest & 1 \\
HerbaceousVegetation & HerbaceousVegetation & 2 \\
Highway & Highway & 3 \\
Industrial, Residential & Urban & 4 \\
River, SeaLake & WaterBodies & 5 \\

Training setup was pretty standard: AdamW optimizer, learning rate of
$3 \times 10^{-4}$ with cosine annealing, batch size 32. We ran it for
15 epochs and used early stopping.

Because collapsing 10 classes down to 6 creates massive class
imbalances, we used Focal Loss (Lin et al., 2017) to keep the gradients
focused. CutMix and Mixup (Yun et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2018) were
essential here. They force the model to actually learn structural
features rather than just memorising colour signatures. That's really
the only way the model survives the jump to a new geographic region.

\subsection{The Sliding Window}

If you just chop the image into an $8 \times 4$ grid, you get ugly
artifacts anywhere a forest or road crosses a tile boundary. The model
lacks spatial context at the edges and hallucinates weird predictions.

Instead, we slide a 224 px window across the image with a stride of 168
px. This gives a 25\% overlap between windows.

\begin{verbatim}
X steps: 0, 168, 336 ... up to 1,512.
Anchor at 1,568 to catch the right edge. That's 11 positions.
Y steps: 0, 168, 336, 504, 672. That's 5 positions.
Total patches: 11 x 5 = 55.
\end{verbatim}

We dump the softmax outputs into a big $[1{,}792 \times 896 \times 6]$
array, adding them up wherever the windows overlap. Then we just take
the argmax for each pixel. It naturally smooths out the class
boundaries.

On a basic Intel i5 laptop, the whole 55-patch sweep finishes in 3 to
4.5 seconds. Peak memory sits around 320 MB. No server GPUs involved.

\subsection{Uncertainty Sampling}

We run a Shannon Entropy check on every patch:

\begin{verbatim}
H(p)      = -sum(p(c) * log(p(c)))
H_norm(p) = H(p) / log(6)
\end{verbatim}

If $H_\text{norm}$ hits 0, the model is absolutely sure about one class. If
it's 1, the model is completely guessing. Any patch scoring above 0.6
gets flagged with a warning in the UI, pointing the expert right at the
hardest Indian domain biases.

\subsection{Building IndiaSat}

When an expert logs a correction, we push this to Firestore:

\begin{verbatim}
{
  "timestamp": "2026-09-11T12:00:00Z",
  "chunk_id": "lat_lon_zoom",
  "ai_prediction": "Forest",
  "human_label": "Urban",
  "confidence": 0.85
}
\end{verbatim}

That bounding box is all we need. At training time, Earth Engine
re-renders the patch. In our first cycle across 53 coordinates, we
collected \textbf{222 corrections}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{assets/firestore_db.png}
\caption{A look at the IndiaSat dataset in Firestore. No pixel data,
just coordinates and labels.}
\label{fig:firestore}
\end{figure}

To put that in perspective: storing 100,000 physical JPEG patches takes
roughly 50 GB. Storing 100,000 IndiaSat bounding boxes takes less than
100 MB.

\subsection{Fine-Tuning the Model}

We took the EuroSAT weights and updated everything, but with a very
careful touch. We set the Adam learning rate down to $5 \times 10^{-5}$.
This lets the model learn Indian spectral signatures without totally
forgetting what a European forest looks like.

We used standard CrossEntropyLoss and trained for 10 epochs with a batch
size of 16.

One critical detail: we had to use experience replay. We mixed a curated
set of original EuroSAT images into the fine-tuning batches alongside
the IndiaSat corrections. If you don't do this, the model completely
collapses toward whichever class you corrected the most.

After training, we just quantized it back to INT8 and shipped it to the
browser.

\subsection{The Version 2 Disaster}

Our first fine-tuning attempt (Version 2) was an interesting failure. We
didn't use experience replay. Because the expert spent most of their
time correcting Forest false-negatives (like tree-lined streets), the
training set got heavily skewed. The model just collapsed and started
predicting Forest for everything.

We actually caught this inside the GeoScan UI while reviewing a
prediction, not from some offline spreadsheet. It was a great sanity
check for the Human-in-the-Loop workflow. We brought in EuroSAT anchors
for Version 3, balanced the classes, and fixed the collapse.

\section{Takeaways}

\subsection{Aggregate Accuracy Hides Real Problems}

The biggest lesson here is that overall accuracy metrics are terrible at
telling you if a model is actually ready for a new geographic region.
92.38\% sounds great until you realize you're misclassifying a quarter
of the water supply. Any team deploying EuroSAT models for local
monitoring absolutely has to audit class-specific performance first.
GeoScan just makes that audit process fast and actionable.

\subsection{The Forest vs.\ Algae Problem}

We still had 14 Forest errors in Version 3. Almost all of them happened
when the model confused dense green forest canopy for a pond completely
choked with algae. In standard RGB, they look exactly the same. Throwing
more labels at the model won't fix it. The real solution is to bring in
Sentinel-2's SWIR bands (B11 and B12), which easily tell water and
vegetation apart. We skipped them here to stay compatible with EuroSAT,
but that's the clear next step.

\subsection{Mode Collapse is Sneaky}

Our Version 2 stumble highlighted a funny quirk about active learning.
If you only correct the model when it fails on one specific class, your
training set becomes incredibly lopsided. You have to mix in old,
balanced data (experience replay) to keep the model grounded. The nice
thing was that the GeoScan UI made it glaringly obvious when the model
collapsed. We saw it immediately without waiting for a test suite to
finish.

\subsection{What We Missed}

We locked everything to 2023 imagery. Indian landscapes change fast.
Crops rotate, things flood, new buildings go up. We didn't build
temporal updates into the bounding boxes yet. Cloud cover was also a
headache. We had to drop 8 coordinates during evaluation just because it
was too cloudy, especially around the monsoon season in places like
Kerala. Also, one person labelled everything. Bringing in multiple
annotators to measure agreement would tighten up the dataset.

\clearpage
\section{Conclusion}

GeoScan proves that top-line accuracy numbers obscure serious,
systematic biases when you move satellite models to new regions. The
EuroSAT baseline did fine on paper but completely face-planted on Indian
rivers because of sediment and algae.

By throwing just one cycle of entropy-guided active learning at the
problem, we cleaned it up. Overall accuracy jumped to 97.21\% on our
final test set, and the massive 22.8\% WaterBodies error rate vanished
completely. We did all this right in the browser using WebAssembly. No
servers, no GPUs.

The IndiaSat coordinate dataset proves you don't need to hoard terabytes
of physical imagery to build good ground-truth data. As long as you have
an API like Earth Engine to render things on demand, bounding boxes are
plenty. Moving forward, the clear next steps are pulling in SWIR bands
to fix the final algae confusions, testing multi-annotator setups, and
maybe migrating to WebGPU to get inference running even faster.
